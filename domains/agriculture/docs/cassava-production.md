About this cassava guide: this advice comes from "Growing Cassava in Nigeria", a guide by the USAID ICS-Nigeria project with IITA. It covers the rain forest and savanna zones of Nigeria. For advice on your own farm, ask your state ADP extension office.

Choosing land for cassava, according to the IITA guide: choose well-drained, deep, loamy soil, and avoid very sandy and very clayey soils. Plant on the flat where the soil is deep and drains well. Make ridges on shallow and clayey soils, and ridges or mounds where the soil gets waterlogged.

Choosing cassava varieties, according to the IITA guide: choose varieties that resist pests and diseases and suit buyers' needs (gari, fufu, flour, starch). The guide names TMS 30572, 4(2)1425, 92/0326 and NR 8082 as commonly grown improved varieties.

Where to get cassava stems, according to the IITA guide: from the National Seed Service, state ADP offices, the Cassava Growers Association and stem out-growers. Stems come in bundles of 50 stems, each 1 metre long; about 50 bundles plant one hectare.

Choosing and keeping cassava stems, according to the IITA guide:
1. Take cuttings from plants 8 to 18 months old. The hard and semi-hard parts of the stem are best; the soft shoot tips often die.
2. Cut stems with a sharp cutlass or secateur and avoid bruising them. Cuttings should be 20 to 25 cm long with 5 or more buds (nodes).
3. Plant cuttings soon after cutting. If you must keep them for 3 to 5 days, put them in clear plastic bags or cover them with plastic under shade.
4. To keep whole stems for longer, stand the bundles upright under shade with the bottom end touching moist soil, and keep the area weed-free. This way stems can be kept for more than 3 months.

When to plant cassava, according to the IITA guide: plant as soon as the rains become steady in your area. The guide gives these times:
1. Rain forest zone: March to November.
2. Derived savanna: April to August.
3. Southern Guinea savanna: May to July.
4. Northern Guinea savanna: July to August.

How to plant cassava cuttings, according to the IITA guide: in sandy soils, plant upright with two-thirds of the cutting in the soil and buds facing up. In shallow and clayey soils, plant at a slant (about 45 degrees) with two-thirds buried; this makes harvesting easier.

Spacing for cassava, according to the IITA guide: the best number is 10,000 plants per hectare (1 m by 1 m). Because some plants are lost, the guide recommends planting 12,300 plants per hectare at 0.9 m by 0.9 m. Spacing changes if cassava is intercropped; good intercrops include maize, melon, groundnut and cowpea.

Weeding cassava, according to the IITA guide: plant soon after land preparation, before weeds come up. Weed with hoes or cutlasses 3 or more times; the forest zone needs more weeding than the savanna. If you use herbicides, follow the label and wear protective clothing, and do not spray when rain is likely.

Fertiliser for cassava, according to the IITA guide: ideally base fertiliser on a soil test. Land full of Chromolaena (Akintola taku) can grow cassava well without fertiliser, while spear grass or poor vegetation shows fertiliser is needed.
1. Forest zone, continuous cropping: apply 200 kg (4 bags) of NPK 15:15:15 per hectare, or a full small matchbox per plant, at 4 to 6 weeks after planting (June to July). Then apply 100 kg of muriate of potash per hectare, or a half-full small matchbox per plant, at 14 to 16 weeks after planting (September).
2. Savanna zone: apply 200 kg (4 bags) of NPK 15:15:15 per hectare, or a full small matchbox per plant, at 4 to 6 weeks after planting, and a second dose of 50 kg of muriate of potash per hectare.
3. Put the fertiliser in holes 5 cm deep and 10 cm from the plant. Do not apply fertiliser when the soil is dry.

Harvesting cassava, according to the IITA guide: roots can be harvested 9 to 18 months after planting. Harvest only when you have a ready market. Avoid bruising the roots, because bruised roots spoil fast. Process roots as soon as possible, not later than 48 hours after harvest.

Cassava diseases, according to the IITA guide: cassava mosaic disease makes leaves small, wrinkled and yellow, and is spread by whiteflies. Plant resistant varieties, pull out sick plants early, and intercrop with maize. For cassava bacterial blight (wet leaf spots, then leaf fall), plant resistant varieties and prune or uproot and burn infected young plants.

Termites in cassava, according to the IITA guide: plant when the soil is moist, pre-sprout cuttings in plastic bags for 3 to 5 days, and keep fields free of dead wood.
