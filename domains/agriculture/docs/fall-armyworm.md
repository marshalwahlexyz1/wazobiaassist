What fall armyworm is, according to FAO: fall armyworm is an insect pest that came from the Americas and has spread across Africa since 2016. The caterpillar (the young stage) does the damage. It eats maize most of all, but also rice, sorghum, vegetables and cotton. The adult moth can fly up to 100 km in one night. FAO says it cannot be wiped out of Africa, so farmers must learn to manage it.

Fall armyworm in Nigeria, according to IITA: it was first reported in Oyo and Ogun states in 2016. IITA says it is now present in all states of Nigeria and the FCT, and some farmers have lost their whole maize crop.

How to recognise the fall armyworm caterpillar, according to FAO: look for an upside-down Y-shaped pale mark on the front of the dark head. Near the tail end there are four dark spots that form a square. The back has 3 yellow stripes. Young caterpillars are pale; older ones turn brown to green and darker.

Signs of fall armyworm damage on maize, according to FAO: young caterpillars scrape the underside of leaves and leave see-through patches called "windows". Older caterpillars hide inside the whorl (the funnel of young leaves) and make ragged holes in the leaves. You will see caterpillar droppings that look like sawdust. In older plants the caterpillar can eat into the cob. Feeding on young plants can kill the growing point.

How fall armyworm breeds, according to FAO: the moth lays eggs in groups of 100 to 200, usually on the underside of leaves near the base of the plant, covered with scales. The eggs hatch in 2 to 3 days. The caterpillar stage lasts 12 to 20 days, then the caterpillar goes into the soil to turn into a moth.

How to prevent fall armyworm, according to FAO:
1. Use good quality seed that germinates well.
2. Do not plant late, and avoid planting fields at different times in the same area. The last-planted field attracts many moths. FAO calls this one of the most important recommendations for smallholders.
3. Keep the soil healthy. Too much nitrogen fertiliser can make moths lay more eggs.
4. Mix crops. Maize grown with cassava, yams or other crops may attract fewer moths, and plant diversity helps the insects that kill fall armyworm.

How to check your maize field, according to FAO: in the first 40 days after planting, walk through the field every 3 to 4 days. To measure how bad the attack is:
1. Walk across the field in the shape of the letter "W".
2. At the start and at each turn, check 10 plants in a row (5 stops, 50 plants in total).
3. Count a plant as infested only if the whorl has fresh leaf damage or fresh droppings.
4. Add up the infested plants and multiply by 2 to get the percentage of plants infested.

What to do by hand, according to FAO: crush egg masses between your fingers and pick off young caterpillars before they go deep into the whorl. FAO says to visit fields twice a week during the leafy growth stage. Some farmers report putting ash, sand or soil into the whorl, or using neem or hot pepper extracts, but FAO says these have had little scientific study and should be tested by farmers.

Natural enemies of fall armyworm, according to FAO: ants, earwigs, wasps and diseases of the caterpillar kill many fall armyworms; studies found up to 56% of caterpillars killed this way. Many pesticides kill these "farmers' friends" too. FAO also says maize can recover from a lot of leaf damage, so do not panic at the first holes.

Using insecticides on fall armyworm, according to FAO: use only pesticides that are registered in Nigeria. Avoid highly hazardous pesticides, and avoid products with red or yellow labels unless they can be used safely. The spray must reach the caterpillar inside the whorl. Spraying early in the season may kill natural enemies and may not pay for itself. Follow the label and wear protective clothing.

Insecticide timing in northern Nigeria, according to IITA: some insecticides on the Nigerian market can control fall armyworm if applied in the first 3 weeks after planting maize. IITA names a product with lambda-cyhalothrin plus dimethoate, applied once a week for 3 weeks after planting. Follow the label and wear protective clothing, and ask your ADP extension officer which products are approved.

Fall armyworm and aflatoxin, according to FAO: damage from fall armyworm does not by itself make maize unsafe to eat, but it could make the maize more likely to have aflatoxin. Remove damaged cobs and dry and store grain well.
