What causes aflatoxin, according to IITA: aflatoxin contamination of maize and groundnut is caused by fungi (moulds) that infect the crop. The IITA "Farmers' Guide to Management of Aflatoxin in Maize and Groundnuts in Africa" says crops grown under stress are more easily infected by these fungi, and that insect damage makes wounds that help the fungi get in. Good farming before and after harvest reduces aflatoxin.

Before harvest: how to reduce aflatoxin in maize and groundnut, according to IITA:
1. Use improved varieties recommended for your area, and ask extension agents for advice.
2. Get seed and other inputs from a reliable source, such as an agro-dealer.
3. Plant on time, so the crop has enough rain to grow and mature. Do not plant so early that the crop matures during the rains.
4. Apply fertiliser and other key inputs. This reduces crop stress.
5. Weed on time, with a hoe, bull, tractor or herbicide.
6. Control insects, especially stem borer, during crop growth.
7. IITA's guide recommends applying Aflasafe, a product with beneficial fungus strains, at the right crop stage: after rains, when rain is expected, or when the soil is wet. Ask your ADP extension officer if it is approved and available in your area, and follow the label.

At harvest: how to reduce aflatoxin, according to IITA:
1. Harvest as soon as the crop is mature. Timely harvest limits aflatoxin.
2. Keep plants standing upright. Lift maize plants that have fallen on the ground and tie them together. Do not let cobs touch the soil.
3. Separate immature, insect-damaged or diseased cobs from good ones. Damaged cobs may have high aflatoxin.
4. If you heap maize plants in the field to dry, keep the heaps standing upright in a cone shape so they do not fall down.
5. Remove the husks straight into a bag or container, not onto the floor.

Drying grain to reduce aflatoxin, according to IITA:
1. Never dry grain on bare ground, in the field or at home. Dry it on plastic (polyethylene) sheets or on a raised platform.
2. Keep the crop away from water and moisture while it dries.
3. While drying, keep removing immature, infected, broken and damaged cobs and dirt. Do not dry sick-looking grain together with healthy grain.
4. To test if maize is dry, crack a kernel between your teeth. If it shatters, it is dry. If it is sticky, it is not dry yet.

How dry maize should be for storage, according to IITA's maize guide for northern Nigeria: dry maize to 10 to 12% moisture before storage. Grain must be below 12% moisture to avoid mould.

Threshing, storing and moving grain to reduce aflatoxin, according to IITA:
1. Use a well-set (calibrated) thresher. Do not beat maize with sticks; this breaks more kernels.
2. Keep the threshing place clean so grain does not touch soil or animal dirt.
3. Clean, repair and disinfect the store before bringing in the new harvest. IITA's guide also advises treating the store against insects and rats; if you use chemicals, follow the label and wear protective clothing.
4. Do not put bags on bare ground or against walls.
5. Carry grain in a covered, waterproof vehicle so it does not get wet again.

Storage pests of maize, according to IITA's maize guide for northern Nigeria: the main storage pests are grain weevils. They can come from old grain left in the store, from other stores nearby, or from the field if maize is left too long before harvest. Keep the store or granary very clean before and during storage. For storage without chemicals, IITA advises airtight triple-layer storage bags.

When to ask for help: if you are not sure whether your grain is safe, talk to your state ADP extension officer.
