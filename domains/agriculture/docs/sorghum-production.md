About this sorghum guide: this advice comes from the ICRISAT "Handbook on Improved Agronomic Practices for Sorghum Production in North East Nigeria", written for Borno and Adamawa states. Sorghum is also called guinea corn or dawa. Farmers in other areas should ask their state ADP extension office.

When to plant sorghum in North East Nigeria, according to ICRISAT: plant after the rains have arrived and there is enough moisture in the soil. Sorghum is normally planted from the end of May in southern Adamawa State to the end of June or early July in northern Borno State, depending on the place and the variety.

Soil and land preparation for sorghum, according to ICRISAT: sorghum grows best on deep, fertile, well-drained loamy soil, but it can also grow on poorer and sandy soils. Use land that was left fallow for two or more years or had legumes the season before. ICRISAT recommends working 2 to 5 tonnes per hectare of farmyard manure into the soil at ploughing. For normal tillage, plough or harrow and make ridges 75 cm apart.

Choosing sorghum varieties in North East Nigeria, according to ICRISAT: choose a variety whose growing period suits the rains in your area. ICRISAT lists early varieties for the Sudan and Sahel savanna, such as SAMSORG 40, SAMSORG 41, SAMSORG 45, SAMSORG 46 and SAMSORG 49, and medium varieties for the Northern Guinea savanna, such as SAMSORG 47 and SAMSORG 48. SAMSORG 17, 40, 41 and 49 are listed as Striga tolerant.

Seed rate and spacing for sorghum, according to ICRISAT: 8 to 10 kg of seed plants one hectare of sorghum alone. Sow 3 to 5 seeds per hole, with 75 cm between rows and 25 to 30 cm between holes. Plant about 5 cm deep, and never deeper than 5 cm. Dress all seed before planting; ICRISAT says one sachet of mancozeb or another seed dressing is enough for 4 kg (2 mudus) of seed. Follow the label and wear protective clothing.

Thinning sorghum, according to ICRISAT: thin to two plants per hill two weeks after sowing. Fill gaps by transplanting when the soil is wet, preferably in the evening.

Fertiliser for sorghum, according to ICRISAT: apply 4 bags (200 kg) of NPK 15:15:15 per hectare at planting and 2 bags (100 kg) of urea per hectare before flowering, when the soil is moist. Cover the fertiliser with soil.

Fertiliser micro-dosing for sorghum, according to ICRISAT: this means putting small amounts of fertiliser close to each plant, which saves fertiliser. The steps are:
1. 5 to 10 days after the seedlings come up, bury 2 bottle corks full of NPK 15:15:15 in a hole about 5 cm deep near each hill.
2. 4 to 5 weeks after sowing, apply 1 bottle cork full of urea.
3. Instead, you can use manure: one handful (100 to 200 g) of cow, sheep or goat dung per hill, or 50 to 100 g of poultry manure per hill.

Weeding sorghum, according to ICRISAT: weed control matters most in the first six weeks after sowing. Weed 2 to 3 times, every 3 to 4 weeks, depending on how many weeds there are.

Striga in sorghum, according to ICRISAT: Striga is a parasitic weed with bright red flowers that attaches to sorghum roots. The sorghum leaves wilt, roll and look scorched even when the soil has water. Striga seeds can stay alive in the soil for 15 to 20 years. To reduce Striga, rotate sorghum with cotton, groundnut, cowpea or pigeon pea, pull Striga plants out before they flower, and use tolerant varieties.

Insect pests of sorghum, according to ICRISAT: early planting helps sorghum escape spittle bug, shoot fly, stem borer and sorghum midge. Plant early and at about the same time over a large area. After harvest, burn or plough in dry stalks and stubble to reduce stem borers.

Harvesting sorghum in North East Nigeria, according to ICRISAT: in Borno State, early varieties are usually ready in October and medium varieties in November. In Adamawa State, early and medium varieties are harvested by the end of November and late varieties in early December. To check if the grain is mature, pull out a few grains: if the point where the grain was attached is brownish-black, it is mature. Harvest straight away, because some varieties shed their grain.

Storing sorghum, according to ICRISAT: about 35% of sorghum is lost to insects in storage, mainly grain moths and weevils. Store grain in airtight containers or triple-layer airtight bags. The grain must be clean and dried to below 12% moisture. A simple test: break a few grains with your teeth. If they break with a cracking sound, the grain is dry enough to store.
