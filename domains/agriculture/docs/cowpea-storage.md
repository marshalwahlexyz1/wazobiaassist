Why stored cowpea gets destroyed, according to Purdue University: stored cowpea (beans) is attacked by small insects called bruchids, often known as cowpea weevils. The attack starts in the field at a low level. In the store, the female lays eggs on the cowpea. The eggs hatch in about a week, and each young grub bores into a seed, where it feeds and grows for about four weeks. A new adult then comes out, leaving a small round hole, and starts laying eggs again. After two or three months, a store that had only a few weevils can contain thousands, and the grain can turn into smelly, worthless powder.

Why many farmers sell cowpea at harvest, according to Purdue University: many farmers sell their cowpea at harvest because they know the weevils will destroy it if they store it. Purdue and African partners developed a simple storage method without insecticides or other chemicals: airtight (hermetic) storage in triple-layer plastic bags, known as PICS bags.

What the triple-layer storage bag is, according to Purdue University: the bag has two thick inner plastic bags (liners) inside a strong outer woven sack. The inner liners stop air from passing through. The outer woven sack makes the bag easy to handle.

How airtight bags stop weevils, according to Purdue University: once the bag is tightly closed, the insects inside use up the oxygen. When the oxygen is too low, they stop eating, stop growing and stop breeding, so the damage stops. The insects then dry up and die. Purdue reports that cowpea kept this way stays as good as when it was put in the bag, even after several months.

Before you use a triple-layer storage bag, according to Purdue University:
1. Make sure the bag is genuine and not a copy. The two inner liners should be thick and fairly hard to see through.
2. Take the three bags apart. Check both inner liners for holes and tears, and check that their bottoms are well sealed. Do not use a bag with holes.
3. Make sure the cowpea is completely dry and clean, with no rubbish in it.

How to fill and close the triple-layer storage bag, according to Purdue University:
1. Pour a small amount of grain into the first inner liner so it is easier to put into the second liner. Then put both liners into the woven sack.
2. Fold the tops of the three bags together, inside-out.
3. Fill the inner bag with grain, shaking gently to remove air pockets. Do not let any grain fall between the bags.
4. Leave enough space at the top for tying. Pack the grain tightly to remove air.
5. Twist the top of the innermost liner tightly, fold the twist over and tie it firmly with string.
6. Tuck the knot in, then twist, fold and tie the second liner the same way.
7. Finally, tie the outer woven sack. Tie each bag separately.

Caring for stored bags, according to Purdue University:
1. Keep the bags away from sunlight and strong heat, which can damage the woven sack.
2. Keep the bags on a raised platform and away from walls, so you can check them and rats are less likely to attack.
3. Check the bags at least once a month.
4. Keep the bag sealed all the time. If you take out grain for food or sale, press out the extra air and tie the bag again straight away.

Common questions about airtight cowpea storage, according to Purdue University:
1. Should I add insecticide to the bag? No, there is no need.
2. Can seed stored in the bag still grow? Yes, but dry the grain below 13% moisture before storage to keep the seed alive.
3. How long can grain stay in the bag? Grain can be stored for a year or more.
4. Can the bags be used again? Yes, for about three seasons on average, as long as the liners have no holes.
5. Can I store small amounts or different crops? Yes. A large bag can hold a smaller amount, and you can put each kind of grain in its own small bag inside the big bag.

Other crops that can be stored this way, according to Purdue University: the bags were first made for cowpea, but they also work against storage insects in maize, beans, sorghum, pigeon pea, groundnut (peanuts) and rice.
