What diarrhoea is, from the World Health Organization (WHO): diarrhoea means passing 3 or more loose or watery stools (poo) in one day, or passing stool more often than is normal for that person. Passing normal formed stools often is not diarrhoea. Loose, pasty stools in breastfed babies are also not diarrhoea.

Why diarrhoea is dangerous for children, from WHO: diarrhoea is the third leading cause of death in children under 5 years old, but it can be prevented and treated. Diarrhoea can last several days and can leave the body without the water and salts it needs to survive. The most serious danger is dehydration, which happens when the water and salts lost in stool, vomit, sweat, urine and breathing are not replaced. Children who are malnourished or have weak immunity are most at risk.

Danger signs of dehydration in a child with diarrhoea, from WHO: WHO lists these signs of severe dehydration: very sleepy, weak or unconscious; sunken eyes; not able to drink or drinking poorly; and skin that goes back very slowly (2 seconds or more) after it is pinched. WHO uses at least two of these signs to mean severe dehydration. Signs that a child has lost some water are being restless or irritable, sunken eyes, and drinking eagerly because of thirst.

If a child with diarrhoea has any danger sign, such as being very sleepy or unconscious, sunken eyes, not able to drink, blood in the stool, or diarrhoea that has lasted 14 days or more, do not wait at home. Go to the nearest health facility or primary health centre (PHC) now. A newborn baby with diarrhoea should be taken to the hospital immediately, according to WHO's guide for newborn care.

ORS for diarrhoea, from WHO: WHO says diarrhoea should be treated with oral rehydration salts (ORS) solution. ORS is a mixture of clean water, salt and sugar. It replaces the water and salts the body loses in diarrhoea, and it is cheap. Ask a health worker how to prepare and give ORS.

Zinc for diarrhoea, from WHO: WHO says zinc supplements, given as dispersible zinc tablets, shorten diarrhoea and reduce the amount of stool. A health worker can give zinc and explain how to use it.

Keep feeding during diarrhoea, from WHO: keep giving nutritious food, including breast milk, while the child has diarrhoea. Diarrhoea and poor nutrition make each other worse, and feeding the child well helps break this cycle.

When to see a health worker, from WHO: WHO advises seeing a health professional, especially when diarrhoea lasts a long time (14 days or more), when there is blood in the stool, or when there are signs of dehydration. Children with severe dehydration or shock may need fluids through a drip (into a vein) at a health facility.

Preventing diarrhoea, from WHO: the main ways to prevent diarrhoea are safe drinking water, using a good toilet or latrine, washing hands with soap, giving only breast milk for the first 6 months of life, good personal and food hygiene, learning how infections spread, and rotavirus vaccination. Diarrhoea is often spread through food or water dirtied with human or animal faeces (poo), or from person to person when hygiene is poor.
