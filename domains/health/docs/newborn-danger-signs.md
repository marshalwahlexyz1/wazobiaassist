Danger signs in a newborn baby, from the World Health Organization (WHO): WHO says families should seek medical care quickly if the newborn has feeding problems, is less active than normal, has difficult breathing, has a fever, has fits or convulsions, or feels cold.

When to go to the hospital immediately with a new baby, from WHO's guide for health workers on pregnancy, childbirth, postpartum and newborn care: the mother should return or go to the hospital immediately if the baby has difficulty breathing, convulsions (fits), fever, feels cold, has bleeding, has diarrhoea, is very small and just born, or is not feeding at all.

When to go to the health centre as quickly as possible with a new baby, from the same WHO guide: the mother should take the baby to the health centre as quickly as possible if the baby has difficulty feeding, pus coming from the eyes, pus spots (pustules) on the skin, yellow skin, a cord stump that is red or has pus coming out, or if the baby feeds fewer than 5 times in 24 hours.

If a newborn baby has any danger sign, do not wait at home. Go to the nearest health facility or primary health centre (PHC) now, day or night. If the baby has difficulty breathing, fits, fever, feels cold, is bleeding, has diarrhoea or is not feeding at all, go to the hospital immediately.

Keeping the baby warm on the way to the hospital, from WHO: during the journey, keep the baby warm with skin-to-skin contact with the mother or someone else. Cover the baby with a blanket and cover the head with a cap. Protect the baby from direct sunshine. Keep breastfeeding during the journey. If possible, the mother should go with the baby.

Why the first days of life matter, from WHO: the first month of life is the time when a child is most at risk of dying. Most newborn deaths happen in the first week of life, and about 1 million newborns die within the first 24 hours. WHO says few women and newborns stay in the facility for the recommended 24 hours after birth, even though this is the most important time when problems can appear. Too many newborns die at home because they leave the hospital too early, cannot reach care, or the family waits too long to seek care.

Care that every newborn baby should get, according to WHO: keep the baby warm, for example with skin-to-skin contact between mother and baby; keep the cord and skin clean; start breastfeeding early and give only breast milk; have the baby checked for signs of serious health problems; give the baby the vaccines due at birth; register the birth; and bring the baby for vaccines on time according to the national schedule.

Breastfeeding a newborn, from WHO's guide for health workers: babies should start breastfeeding within 1 hour of birth and should not get any other food or drink before they start to breastfeed. Babies should get only breast milk for the first 6 months of life.

Babies born very small, from WHO: if a low-birth-weight baby is found at home, the family should be helped to find a hospital or health facility to care for the baby. Small babies need extra attention to keeping warm, help with breastfeeding, extra hand washing, and extra attention to danger signs.
