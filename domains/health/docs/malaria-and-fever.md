What malaria is, from the World Health Organization (WHO): malaria is a life-threatening disease spread to people by the bites of some infected female mosquitoes. It is caused by a parasite and does not spread from person to person. Malaria can be prevented and it can be cured. Blood transfusion and dirty needles can also spread malaria.

Early signs of malaria, from WHO: the most common early signs of malaria are fever, headache and chills. Signs usually start 10 to 15 days after a person is bitten by an infected mosquito. The first signs can be mild and can look like many other illnesses with fever, so it can be hard to know that it is malaria. WHO says that because the signs are not specific, getting tested early is important.

Children under 5 and malaria, from WHO: babies, children under 5 years, pregnant women, travellers and people with HIV or AIDS are at higher risk of severe malaria. In the WHO African Region, children under 5 years make up about three quarters of all malaria deaths. WHO says Nigeria had the largest share of malaria deaths in the African Region in 2024.

Danger signs of severe malaria, from WHO: severe signs include extreme tiredness, being confused or not fully awake, many convulsions (fits), difficulty breathing, dark or bloody urine, yellow eyes and skin (jaundice), and unusual bleeding. WHO says people with severe signs should get emergency care right away. Without treatment, the most dangerous type of malaria can become severe illness and lead to death within 24 hours.

If a child or adult with fever has any danger sign, such as fits, difficulty breathing, being very sleepy or confused, dark or bloody urine, yellow eyes, or unusual bleeding, do not wait at home. Go to the nearest health facility or primary health centre (PHC) now. A newborn baby with fever, or a baby who feels cold, should be taken to the hospital immediately, according to WHO's guide for newborn care.

Test before treatment, from WHO: WHO recommends that everyone who may have malaria is tested to confirm it before treatment, using either a microscope test or a rapid diagnostic test (RDT). Early testing and treatment reduces illness, prevents deaths and helps stop malaria spreading. Getting treatment early for mild malaria can stop it from becoming severe.

Treatment of malaria, from WHO: malaria is a serious infection and always needs treatment with medicine. A health worker chooses the medicine based on the type of malaria, the age or weight of the person, and whether the person is pregnant. Some people may need to go to a health centre or hospital for medicine by injection. Go to a health worker or primary health centre (PHC) for testing and the right medicine.

Mosquito nets and other ways to prevent malaria, from WHO: sleep under a mosquito net in places where malaria is present. WHO says insecticide-treated nets are one of the two main ways to control the mosquitoes that spread malaria; the other is spraying insecticide on the inside walls of houses. Other ways to avoid mosquito bites are using mosquito repellent after dark, using coils and vaporisers, wearing clothing that covers the body, and using window screens.

Malaria in pregnancy, from WHO: malaria during pregnancy can cause the baby to be born too early or born with low birth weight. Pregnant women are at higher risk of severe malaria. WHO's guide for health workers says a pregnant woman with fever should go to the health centre as soon as possible, and a pregnant woman with fever who is too weak to get out of bed should go to the hospital or health centre immediately, day or night.
