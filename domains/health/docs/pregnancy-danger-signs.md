Severe danger signs in pregnancy, from the World Health Organization (WHO): a pregnant woman should go to the hospital immediately if she has any of these signs. Bleeding from the vagina. Convulsions or fits. Severe headache with blurred vision. Fever and being too weak to get out of bed. Severe pain in the belly (abdomen). Fast breathing or difficult breathing. WHO says health workers should tell every pregnant woman, at every antenatal visit, how important it is to go to the hospital immediately if any of these severe danger signs appear.

If a pregnant woman has any danger sign, she should not wait at home. She should go to the nearest health facility or primary health centre (PHC) now. If the sign is severe, such as bleeding, fits, severe headache with blurred vision, severe belly pain, or difficult breathing, she should go to the hospital immediately.

Other danger signs in pregnancy, from WHO: a pregnant woman should look out for these signs and seek care at a health facility if she has them. Headache. The baby is not moving. Contractions (the womb tightening). Abnormal discharge from the vagina. Fever. Pain in the belly. Feeling ill. Swollen fingers, swollen face or swollen legs. WHO says health workers should teach pregnant women at every antenatal visit how to look out for these danger signs and get care.

What the health worker checks at every antenatal visit, according to WHO: before each antenatal visit, the health worker does a quick check for danger signs. The signs on this list are: bleeding from the vagina, fits (convulsions), fever, severe headache, problems with seeing, labour or the baby about to be born, looking very ill, severe vomiting, severe pain, severe belly pain, being unconscious, and a bluish colour around the mouth, lips and tongue. WHO says that if the woman has any of these signs, the health worker should refer her to the hospital urgently. If there are no danger signs, the normal antenatal visit continues.

Getting ready for birth and for emergencies, from WHO: health workers should help a pregnant woman plan for the birth and for problems that may happen. The plan includes: a skilled birth attendant; a companion for labour and birth; knowing where the closest health facility is, for the birth and in case of problems; money for costs of the birth and of any problems; the supplies and materials to take to the facility; someone to look after the home and other children while she is away; transport to a facility for the birth or in case of a problem; and finding blood donors who can give blood in case of problems.

Women who should plan to give birth in a health facility, from WHO guidance for health workers: health workers are told to encourage birth at a health facility for a woman who is 17 years old or younger, a woman in her first pregnancy, a woman who has already given birth 6 or more times, a woman who had a caesarean operation before, and a woman who had problems in a past birth such as heavy bleeding, fits, a delivery with forceps or vacuum, or a serious tear during birth. The same applies if she has bleeding from the vagina now, is carrying more than one baby, the baby is not lying in the normal position, she is living with HIV, or she wants a family planning device or operation right after birth.
